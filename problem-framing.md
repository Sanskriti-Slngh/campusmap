# Problem framing

## Domain

In student clubs, a student may spend a year learning how to run one part of the organization, then hand that role to someone who has never done it before. The work itself does not stop when the officer changes. A treasurer may submit reimbursement forms and keep receipts; an events chair may reserve rooms, contact speakers or vendors, and meet deadlines; a president may update officer information and work with advisors.

These jobs are not always easy to learn from official instructions alone. University rules may explain what is required, but officers also pick up practical knowledge through experience: who to contact, how early to start, which steps usually cause delays, and what mistakes to avoid. Clubs want to preserve that knowledge so each new officer does not have to rediscover the same process from scratch.

Because leadership changes often, outgoing officers usually pass what they know to the next person. They may hold a handoff meeting, write instructions in a Google Doc, organize files in a shared Drive, pass along spreadsheets, forward important emails, or explain parts of the job in person.

These tools are good at storing instructions and passing them along. However, the information is often split across files or written as a list of steps. A document might say, “Email the advisor before submitting the form,” without making it clear whether that step comes from a current university rule, a problem the club had in the past, or a workaround that simply made sense at the time.

That matters because the next officer is not just learning a process. They are also deciding what the club should keep doing. A useful handoff should help them take over quickly without repeating old mistakes, while also giving them enough context to understand the procedures they inherited.

This project focuses on how student clubs pass recurring procedures from one group of officers to the next, especially when those procedures outlast the officers who created them.

## Stakeholders

- **Current officers, including newly incoming officers.** They inherit and carry out procedures from previous officers, and when context is missing, they may have to decide whether to keep following a procedure or change it without fully knowing why it exists.

- **Outgoing or former officers.** They often know how a procedure developed and why certain steps were added. They pass that knowledge on during a transition, but missing context becomes harder to recover once they leave.

- **Club members.** They may not maintain the procedures themselves, but their events, purchases, and other club activities can be delayed or made harder when officers follow outdated or unclear processes.

- **University offices and advisors.** They set or communicate rules such as spending requirements, approval steps, and deadlines that some club procedures are built around. When those rules change, the club's written procedure may no longer match the situation it was created for.

## Bad situations

### New officers spend hours relearning a process the team already knows

On a competitive dance team, registering for competitions involves several steps. The competition chair has to keep track of registration deadlines, submit team information, upload music, pay fees, and make sure everything is completed in the right order.

When a new competition chair took over, they had never registered the team for a competition before. There was no clear place where the full process was written down, so they scheduled a call with the previous chair to learn how it worked.

The call lasted about **two hours**. They went through deadlines, the order of submissions, music requirements, fees, and details the previous chair had learned through experience. Even after the call, the new chair still had follow-up questions because some parts of the process had only been explained verbally.

This is not a one-time task. The team enters multiple competitions, and the same registration process comes back every season. The problem also returns when a new person takes over the role. Much of the process stays the same, but the knowledge has to be passed from person to person again.

The cost is more than the two hours spent on one call. Executive members already spend significant time running the team, and relearning these processes adds more work before they can even begin doing the job themselves. This burden has contributed to people leaving executive positions. When someone leaves, the team also loses another person who understood the process, making the next transition harder again.

### The club kept trusting information that was no longer true

A student club had used the same allocated storage space for about **five years**. Each group of officers was told by the previous group that the club had the space, so the information became part of the club's normal handoff. Because several generations of officers had relied on it successfully, there was little reason for a new officer to question whether anything had changed.

During that time, the storage policy changed, and clubs were now required to renew their space. The new requirement was mentioned informally to officers at the time, but it was never added to the club's written handoff information. As leadership changed again, that detail was forgotten, while the older assumption that the club simply had the space continued to be passed down.

The next presidents were not aware that a renewal was required. They only learned about the rule when they were informed that the club had **lost the storage space**.

The club then had about **one week** to move everything out. Officers had to quickly find temporary storage through the Stud while also organizing the move, and at one point were considering keeping the club's belongings at a member's house because no other space was ready.

What made the problem difficult to notice was that most of the handoff appeared to work. The old information had been preserved successfully for years, while the newer information that should have changed the procedure had been passed only informally and disappeared.

The result was that an outdated assumption continued to become more trusted even after the rule behind it had changed.

### The common problem

A handoff can fail because knowledge disappears, but it can also fail because outdated knowledge survives.

On the dance team, useful knowledge does not survive clearly enough, so new officers spend hours relearning a process that the team has already figured out.

In the storage case, the older information survives while the update that should have changed it disappears. As a result, new officers continue trusting an assumption that is no longer true.

Simply passing information from one officer to the next therefore does not guarantee that future officers will have the right information when they need it. A club can lose knowledge that should have survived, or continue trusting knowledge that should have been updated.

## Corroboration

The two bad situations above come from clubs I have been part of, so I looked for the same pattern in other MIT student organizations. I spoke with officers from **SAAS, MIT Mirchi, and StartLabs** about how they learned recurring responsibilities after taking over a role.

In **3 out of 3 clubs**, current officers had contacted previous officers to understand parts of their role after the handoff. All three also had examples of inherited procedures where the current officer knew what to do but did not fully know why it was done that way. The amount of documentation differed: MIT Mirchi had more written processes, while SAAS and StartLabs relied more heavily on information passed directly between officers. Even at Mirchi, however, officers still contacted previous officers for context that the written material did not capture. 

For example, at MIT Mirchi, officers had a spreadsheet with dates, submission links, and requirements for dance videos. Even with that written record, the current officer still contacted a previous officer to figure out which links were still current, which ones referred to older submissions, and how the requirements had been handled in practice. The spreadsheet preserved the steps, but not enough context to confidently use them without asking someone who had done the process before.

Research on organizational turnover supports the first part of this problem. Galan's 2023 systematic review examined **91 empirical studies** of knowledge loss caused by members leaving organizations and found that turnover can result in the loss of several kinds of organizational knowledge.[1] Student organizations were not a major focus of these studies, so the review cannot tell us how often this happens in clubs like mine. But it supports the basic pattern in the dance-team example: when knowledge stays mostly with one person, some of it can leave when that person does.

Writing a process down helps, but the rule can survive more easily than the reason behind it. Vuori, Laamanen, and Zollo found that when organizations turned experience into reusable procedures, some of the causal knowledge—why an action worked in the first place—was lost.[2] Their study looked at corporate acquisitions rather than student clubs, so it does not show how often this happens on campus. It does, however, explain the storage example well: the information survived, but the reason determining whether it was still correct did not.

The consequences of outdated administrative knowledge can also be significant at MIT. MIT's official student-organization policy states that an organization that fails required administrative obligations or re-registration can be de-recognized, immediately losing privileges and access to ASA-allocated space; its financial accounts can also be frozen.[3] This does not show that poor handoffs usually cause these outcomes, but it confirms that missing an administrative requirement can cost a club real resources.

Together, the evidence supports both sides of the problem. Sometimes useful knowledge does not survive clearly enough, forcing the next officer to reconstruct it. Other times the procedure survives while the reasoning behind it becomes unclear or outdated. The local interviews show both patterns in MIT clubs, while the broader research helps explain why they can occur.

## Workarounds and comparables

Student clubs already use several reasonable ways to pass knowledge between officers. **Handoff calls** are useful because former officers can explain details that were never written down and answer questions immediately. In the dance-team example, the handoff call solved the immediate problem, but it required a new officer to spend about two hours relearning a process the team had already figured out. When leadership changes again, much of the same explanation may have to happen again.

**Google Docs, Drive, and spreadsheets** are attractive because clubs already use them and they make information easier to preserve across leadership changes. They can prevent an entire procedure from disappearing with one officer. However, they do not guarantee that the right information survives. In the storage example, the older assumption that the club had the space continued to be passed down, while the newer renewal requirement was mentioned informally and disappeared. A document can therefore preserve a process while still leaving out the update that should have changed it.

More structured tools such as **Notion** and **Confluence** give clubs better ways to organize this information. A club can create pages for recurring procedures, record explanations, keep history, and connect related information. These tools can reduce relearning by putting procedures and explanations in one place, and their history and organization make changes easier to track.

The bad situations can still happen, however. In the dance-team case, a club may still have several pages, spreadsheets, and notes without one clear process that captures the practical knowledge a new officer needs. The new officer can still end up contacting the previous officer to reconstruct what is missing. In the storage case, the problem is even harder: the old information can remain neatly documented while a newer rule is never added, so future officers continue trusting information that is no longer true.

The issue is therefore not simply whether a tool can store procedures or explanations. The problem is that important context can still disappear during a handoff, while older documented information continues to look trustworthy. A club therefore needs some way to preserve the few pieces of context that matter and recognize when changes to that context make inherited procedures worth reconsidering. Relay focuses specifically on that problem rather than trying to replace the club's general-purpose document or wiki tools.

## Solution sketch

A possible solution is a club playbook where procedures and the reasons behind them are stored as separate but connected pieces of information. One rule or assumption could be connected to several procedures, so when an officer learns that it has changed, all of those procedures can be brought back for review. An officer could record a procedure such as registering for a competition or renewing storage space, along with the rule, past experience, or assumption that explains important steps. 

For the dance team, a new competition chair could inherit the registration process and the explanations previous chairs learned through experience. Instead of spending two hours reconstructing the process from a former officer, she would begin with what the team already knows and contact the former chair only for anything that is still unclear.

For the storage-space example, the club's storage procedure could be connected to the rule governing whether the space had to be renewed. When an officer learned that the renewal rule had changed, marking that rule as changed would also bring the storage procedure back for review rather than allowing the old assumption to continue being passed down.

This does not require clubs to replace the way they already work. Officers already write handoff notes, maintain shared documents, and explain procedures to the people replacing them. The proposed solution changes how that information is connected: instead of preserving only the steps, it keeps the steps tied to the reasons that made them correct. This could reduce repeated relearning while also making inherited procedures easier to reconsider when their underlying conditions change.
